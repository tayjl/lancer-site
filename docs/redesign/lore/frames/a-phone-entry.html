Salt and Static

A relief pilot arrives at Halvard Station and finds a dead ferry still asking the shoals to wait.

By Ostrander12 Nov 20263 min read

The relief shuttle docked at Halvard three hours late, and by the time Ines Okafor cleared the airlock she understood that nobody on the station was going to mention it.

Nobody mentioned the quiet, either. Halvard smelled the way every tidal hub smells, of brine in the vents and hot copper and the sweet rot of kelp-filter sludge, and she had expected that. She had not expected the dock to be empty. No klaxons, no apprentices racing barrows along the quay. Only the sea-sound in the walls, and a woman at the end of the ramp holding a clipboard like a shield.

“You’re the relief,” said Cassia Vell. “Good. Come and look at something before you unpack.”

Third watch

The comms room was a closet with one window onto black water. Cassia pulled out the only chair for her and stayed standing. On the board a single channel glowed amber, a band the station’s own logs said had been dead since the Merrow Blackout. It had been broadcasting for eleven days.

“Same message, on the hour,” Cassia said. “I don’t think it’s meant for us. I’d like to know who it’s meant for before the Tidewright Compact decides it’s theirs.”

Transmission, 14:00 station time, repeating: “This is the Saltmarsh, to anyone on the shoals. We are holding at the old marker. We have not moved. We are not lost. Please do not send anyone yet.”

Ines read it twice, then asked the dull question first, because that is what relief pilots are for. “What was the Saltmarsh?”

“A grain ferry. She went down on the shoals forty years ago, and the shoals are the one stretch of this coast nobody has ever got a clean sounding of.”

“So someone is spoofing it.”

“Someone has the old call-signs, the old cipher, and the exact patience of a crew that has been told to wait. That isn’t spoofing.” Cassia finally sat on the edge of the console. “That’s manners.”

Ines wrote three things on her palm before the watch changed:

  • The signal never got weaker, wherever she stood on the dock.
  • It went silent for exactly nine seconds before the last word, every hour.
  • The nine seconds began on the night of the blackout.

The Saltmarsh was not lost. She was waiting.

She slept badly. In the morning she made the first honest entry of her new posting in the station log, and underlined it so that no one could say they hadn’t been told: Relief arrived. Nothing to relieve. Staying.
